## What an argument is

An **argument** is a set of statements where some (the **premises**) are offered as reasons to believe another (the **conclusion**). If nothing is being supported — just a list of facts — it isn't an argument, and the question will ask you what *follows* rather than what's *wrong*.

The whole of Logical Reasoning rests on one move: find the conclusion, find what's offered for it, and ask whether the one actually gets you to the other.

## Finding the conclusion

Three tests, in order of reliability:

1. **The "because" test.** Put the candidate sentence first and attach "because" to another sentence. *"Bike lanes make cycling more dangerous, because injuries rose after they were added."* If that reads naturally, the first part is the conclusion. Reverse it and it sounds absurd.
2. **Indicator words.** *Therefore, thus, so, hence, consequently, it follows that, clearly* signal conclusions. *Because, since, for, after all, given that* signal premises. Reliable, but the LSAT often omits them or buries the conclusion mid-paragraph.
3. **The rebuttal pattern.** *"Some claim X. But Y, because Z."* The conclusion is the "But" sentence — the author's position against someone else's. The opening claim is **context**, not the conclusion.

<KeyIdea>
The conclusion is whatever the rest of the passage is trying to make you believe. Indicator words point at it; the "because" test proves it.
</KeyIdea>

## Two things that are not the conclusion

- **The first sentence.** Often it's background or an opposing view.
- **A premise with a strong word in it.** *"Clearly, the committee met twice"* can be a premise if it's offered as a reason for something else. Strength of tone isn't structure.

## Why this matters for every question type

- *Main Conclusion* questions test it directly.
- *Flaw, Weaken, Strengthen, Assumption* questions are about the gap between premises and conclusion — mis-identify the conclusion and you attack the wrong gap.
- *Most Strongly Supported* and *Must Be True* stimuli usually have **no** conclusion; noticing that tells you the task before you read the stem.

<Callout tone="info" title="Habit to build">
Before reading the answer choices on any argument question, say the conclusion to yourself in your own words. If you can't, you haven't finished reading.
</Callout>
