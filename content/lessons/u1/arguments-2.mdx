## Two separate questions

When you read an argument, keep two questions apart:

1. **Are the premises true?** On the LSAT you almost always accept them. The stimulus says "the survey found X" — fine, it found X.
2. **If the premises are true, how much do they support the conclusion?** This is the only thing most LR questions test.

A conclusion can be true and badly argued. A conclusion can be false and perfectly argued from false premises. You're grading the *link*, not the claim.

## The spectrum

Support isn't on/off. Picture a dial:

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Valid** | If premises are true, the conclusion *cannot* be false | "All tenured faculty have doctorates. Ruiz is tenured. So Ruiz has a doctorate." |
| **Strong** | Premises make the conclusion very likely, not certain | "In 40 of 40 trials the alloy failed at 300°C. It will probably fail at 300°C." |
| **Weak** | Premises are relevant but leave big room | "The restaurant was busy on Friday. It must be profitable." |
| **None** | Premises don't bear on the conclusion | "The CEO is charismatic. Therefore the merger is legal." |

<KeyIdea>
Every argument question is asking where on this dial the argument sits and what would move it. Weaken turns the dial down. Strengthen turns it up. Sufficient Assumption turns it all the way to *valid*. Flaw names why it isn't there already.
</KeyIdea>

## Validity is about form

The valid example above works because of its *shape*: all A are B; x is A; so x is B. Swap in nonsense and it still holds. This is why Unit 1 spends four lessons on conditional and quantifier logic: the hardest LSAT questions are won by seeing the shape under the words.

Conversely, these feel convincing but aren't valid:

- *All A are B. x is B. So x is A.* (All cats are mammals; Rex is a mammal; so Rex is a cat.)
- *Most A are B. Most B are C. So some A are C.* (Possible, not guaranteed — the two "most" groups can miss each other.)

You'll meet both again as named flaws.

## Conclusion strength has to match premise strength

A premise about "some" can't deliver a conclusion about "most." A premise about what happened "in the past decade" can't deliver a conclusion about "always." Watch the words that set the dial:

- **Certainty words:** must, cannot, always, never, all, none, guarantees
- **Probability words:** likely, probably, tends to, usually, most
- **Possibility words:** can, may, some, at least one

A conclusion stronger than its premises is the single most common LSAT gap. The right answer to a Flaw question often just says so in abstract language: *"draws a conclusion that is stronger than its premises warrant."*
