## Why grammar is a scoring skill

Many misses tagged "misread" come down to three mechanical habits: not tracking what a pronoun points to, attaching a modifier to the wrong noun, and gliding past a scope word. None of this is about intelligence. It's about reading the sentence that's there rather than the one you expect.

## Referents: what does "this" point to?

> *The council approved the budget after the mayor's revisions. Critics say this will reduce library funding.*

"This" — the approval? The revisions? The budget? On the LSAT the intended referent is usually the nearest complete idea (the approved, revised budget), but the ambiguity is often the point: an answer choice will assume the other reading. When a stimulus uses *this, that, it, such, these*, say the referent out loud before moving on.

The same goes for *the former / the latter*, *respectively*, and *such* ("such measures" = the measures just named, not measures in general).

## Modifiers: what does the clause attach to?

> *Residents who commute by train more than twice a week report higher satisfaction than those who drive.*

"More than twice a week" modifies *commute by train*, not *report*. The group is frequent train commuters; the comparison is with drivers in general (not frequent drivers). An answer that says "frequent commuters are happier than frequent drivers" has quietly moved the modifier.

Rule: a modifier binds to the closest noun or verb it can sensibly attach to. When two readings are possible, the test wants the one that keeps the comparison symmetric only if the sentence actually says so.

## Scope words: the quiet quantifiers

The words below set the *size* of a claim. The test writers choose them deliberately; wrong answers change them.

| Word | Means | Does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| some | at least one | a few, a minority |
| most | more than half | nearly all |
| many | an unspecified large number | most |
| not all | at least one isn't | none |
| only | nothing else | the main thing |
| tends to | usually | always |
| can | it's possible | it happens |

<KeyIdea>
"Some" on the LSAT includes "all." "Not all" includes "almost all." If you hear these words as everyday English, you'll pick extreme wrong answers and reject correct modest ones.
</KeyIdea>

## Restrictive words that flip meaning

- **only / only if / the only** — restricts. *"Only members may vote"*: voting requires membership (not: all members vote).
- **unless / until / without** — introduces a necessary condition. *"No refund unless receipt"*: refund → receipt.
- **except / other than** — carves out. *"All exhibits except the loans are insured"*: loans may or may not be.
- **respectively** — pairs in order. Two lists, matched position by position.

## A reading protocol for dense sentences

<Steps>
1. Find the main verb and its subject. Everything else is modification.
2. Resolve every pronoun and "such/this/these" to a specific noun.
3. Mark the scope words: some, most, all, only, unless, except.
4. Restate the sentence in plain words — one short clause.
</Steps>

That fourth step is the subject of the next lesson. It's the single most useful habit in Reading Comprehension too.
