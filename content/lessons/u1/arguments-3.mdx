## Beyond premise and conclusion

Real LSAT arguments have more moving parts. Four roles cover almost everything:

| Role | What it does | Typical signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Premise** | supports something | because, since, for, after all |
| **Main conclusion** | supported, supports nothing further | therefore, thus, so; survives the "because" test |
| **Sub-conclusion** | supported *and* then used as support | a "so" sentence that is followed by another "so" |
| **Context / concession** | sets the scene or grants a point, supports nothing | "Some argue…", "Admittedly…", "It is true that…" |

## Sub-conclusions: the two-step argument

*The lab's results could not be replicated. **So the finding is unreliable.** Unreliable findings should not guide policy. **Therefore the proposed regulation should be withdrawn.***

"The finding is unreliable" is concluded from the first sentence and then used, with the third, to reach the last. It is a **sub-conclusion** — the hinge of the argument. Argument Part answers describe it as *"a claim that is supported by one premise and in turn supports the main conclusion"* or *"an intermediate conclusion."*

Why it matters: gaps live on both sides of a hinge. The argument could fail getting *to* the sub-conclusion or *from* it.

## Context: the setup

*Many economists expect the tariff to raise prices.* On its own this is a claim; in an argument that goes on to disagree, it's the view being rejected — **context**. The author doesn't rely on it; the author argues against it. Argument Part answers: *"a position the argument as a whole is directed against,"* *"a claim the argument seeks to undermine."*

A subtle variant: the author may *accept* the context and build on it. *"Economists expect the tariff to raise prices. Since higher prices reduce demand, the tariff will reduce imports."* Here the first sentence is a premise. Test it: does the author *use* it as a reason? If yes, premise. If the author argues against or around it, context.

## Concessions: granting a point

*"Admittedly, the new transit line is expensive. But the long-term savings justify the cost."* The author grants something unfavorable to the conclusion and then overrides it. The concession supports nothing — it's there to be answered. Answer language: *"a consideration the argument acknowledges but discounts,"* *"an objection the argument concedes is partly valid."*

<KeyIdea>
To find a sentence's role, ask two questions: *Does anything support it?* and *Does it support anything?* Yes/yes → sub-conclusion. No/yes → premise. Yes/no → main conclusion. No/no → context or concession.
</KeyIdea>

## The trap in Argument Part questions

The most common wrong answer describes the right structure for the **wrong sentence** — calling a premise "the main conclusion," or the sub-conclusion "a claim the argument rejects." Before reading choices, label the quoted sentence yourself using the two questions above. Then match language, not vibes.
