## The causal argument

Most causal stimuli have the same bones:

1. A **phenomenon** — something observed. *Towns that installed roundabouts saw fewer serious crashes.*
2. A **hypothesis** — a cause offered to explain it. *Roundabouts reduce crash severity.*

The move from 1 to 2 is where the argument lives. The phenomenon is a fact; the hypothesis is one story about why. The LSAT wants to know whether you can see the *other* stories.

## The three rivals

For any claim "A causes B" based on A and B going together:

| Rival | The story | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alternative cause** | Something else, C, produces B (and maybe A) | Towns that install roundabouts also lowered speed limits |
| **Reverse causation** | B causes A | Towns with falling crash rates had budget to redesign roads |
| **Coincidence / sample** | The pairing is chance or an artifact | Four towns, one year — noise |

Every Weaken answer on a causal argument is one of these three (or shows the correlation itself is bogus). Every Strengthen answer rules one out or supplies a mechanism. Every Necessary Assumption on a causal argument is the denial of a rival: *"The towns did not also change speed limits."*

## What supports a causal claim

In rough order of strength:

- **Controlled comparison.** The same thing without A didn't show B. (*Towns that didn't build roundabouts saw no change.*) This is the "no cause, no effect" pattern and the strongest ordinary strengthener.
- **Dose-response.** More A, more B. (*Towns with more roundabouts saw bigger drops.*)
- **Mechanism.** A plausible path from A to B. (*Roundabouts force lower entry speeds.*)
- **Ruling out rivals.** No other change occurred; the sample was large and random; the timing is right (A preceded B).
- **Temporal order** alone is weak: A before B is necessary for A to cause B, not sufficient.

<KeyIdea>
A correlation is a claim about *two things happening together*. A causal claim is a claim about *what would happen if you changed one of them*. Evidence for the second always involves a comparison — real or implied — with the case where the cause is absent.
</KeyIdea>

## Spotting the causal conclusion

Causal language is varied. These all assert causation:

*because of, due to, responsible for, leads to, produces, contributes to, is a factor in, explains, results in, prevents, promotes, the reason for, the effect of*

So does any recommendation that assumes it: *"To reduce crashes, towns should build roundabouts"* presupposes roundabouts cause the reduction.

And these report mere association — notice how a stimulus can slide from one to the other:

*is associated with, correlates with, tends to accompany, is more common among, was followed by*

## The experiment the argument wishes it had

A useful habit: picture the ideal study. Two identical groups; one gets A, one doesn't; measure B. Then ask how the stimulus's evidence falls short of that — the shortfall is the gap, and the answer choices will be about it. (Self-selected groups? No control? Measured B before A?)
