## What an assumption is

An **assumption** is an unstated premise the argument needs. It's the bridge from what was said to what was concluded. Once you see the bridge, most LR question types are the same question in different clothes:

| Task | What you do with the gap |
|---|---|
| Flaw | describe the gap |
| Weaken | show the bridge doesn't hold |
| Strengthen | shore up the bridge |
| Necessary Assumption | name a plank the bridge can't do without |
| Sufficient Assumption | supply a plank that completes the bridge |
| Evaluate | ask the question whose answer tests the bridge |

## Finding the gap: the term shift

The most common gap is a **new term** in the conclusion that never appears in the premises.

> *The new curriculum raised students' test scores. Therefore it improved their understanding of mathematics.*

Premise talks about **test scores**. Conclusion talks about **understanding**. The argument assumes test scores track understanding. Every attack and every defense of this argument goes through that link.

Habit: underline the nouns in the conclusion. Any noun not in the premises is where the assumption lives.

## Finding the gap: the leap in strength

> *Most of the firm's recent hires have advanced degrees. So the next hire will have one too.*

No new terms — but "most" has become "will." The gap is the leap from a pattern to a certainty. Assumption: nothing about the next hire makes it an exception.

## Finding the gap: the alternative story

> *Sales rose after the redesign, so the redesign caused the rise.*

The conclusion claims **causation** from a **sequence**. The gap is every other story that fits the same facts: something else drove sales; sales were rising anyway; coincidence. Click through the alternatives:

Each alternative is a weakener; its denial is a strengthener; its denial is also what the argument *assumes*. That's the whole family in one diagram.

<KeyIdea>
Before you read the choices on any assumption-family question, say the gap in one sentence: "This argument assumes that ___." If you can't, reread the conclusion and look for the new term, the leap in strength, or the alternative story.
</KeyIdea>

## Necessary vs sufficient assumptions

The two assumption question types ask for different planks:

- **Necessary**: something the argument *needs*. If it's false, the argument collapses. Test: negate the answer — does the argument fall apart? Necessary assumptions are usually modest ("at least some test scores reflect understanding," "the redesign was not accompanied by a price cut").
- **Sufficient**: something that, added, *guarantees* the conclusion. Usually strong and sweeping ("anything that raises test scores improves understanding").

A sufficient assumption is often *more* than necessary; a necessary assumption is often *less* than sufficient. The wrong answers on each type are frequently the right answer to the other. We'll drill both separately in Unit 2.

## The negation test, briefly

For Necessary Assumption answers: negate the choice and ask if the argument still works.

- Choice: *"Test scores are at least somewhat related to understanding."* Negated: *"Test scores are not at all related to understanding."* The argument is dead. **Necessary.**
- Choice: *"Test scores are the best measure of understanding."* Negated: *"Test scores are not the best measure."* The argument limps on — scores can still track understanding. **Not necessary.**

Notice the second choice is *too strong*. That's the signature wrong answer on NA questions: an answer the argument would be happy to have but doesn't require.
