## Three quantities

| Word | Logical meaning | Reverses? |
|---|---|---|
| **all** (every, any, no…) | 100% — a conditional, A → B | no |
| **most** | more than half | no |
| **some** | at least one (could be all) | **yes** — "some A are B" = "some B are A" |

"Some" is the only one that reverses. "Most A are B" says nothing about what share of B are A. "All A are B" certainly doesn't mean all B are A.

## The inference table

Two statements that share a term can sometimes combine. The complete list of valid combinations:

| Statement 1 | Statement 2 | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| All A are B | All B are C | **All A are C** |
| Some A are B | All B are C | **Some A are C** |
| Most A are B | All B are C | **Most A are C** |
| All A are B | No B are C | **No A are C** |
| Most B are A | Most B are C | **Some A are C** |
| All B are A | Most B are C | **Some A are C** |
| All B are A | All B are C | **Some A are C** (if any B exist) |

And the combinations that yield **nothing**:

- Some + some (any arrangement)
- Most A are B + Most B are C
- All A are B + Most B are C (the "most" B that are C may be none of the A)
- Some A are B + Some B are C

<KeyIdea>
To chain through a shared term, the shared term needs to be the *subject* of a "most" or inside an "all." "Some" + "some" never chains. "Most" + "most" chains only when both have the same subject.
</KeyIdea>

## Experiment

Set the two statements and watch what follows. Try *most A are B, most B are C* (nothing), then *most B are A, most B are C* (some A are C).

## Why "most + most about the same subject" works

If more than half of B are A, and more than half of B are C, the two groups must overlap: you can't fit two majorities of the same set into disjoint halves. So some B are both A and C — hence some A are C. This shows up in Must Be True questions as a level-3 or level-4 item and in Parallel Reasoning constantly.

## Why "all A are B + most B are C" fails

All A sit inside B, but they could all be in the minority of B that aren't C. Picture B as 100 people, 10 of them A, 60 of them C: the 10 A's can sit among the 40 non-C's. No inference.

## In argument form

> *Most of the museum's acquisitions last year were donations. Donated works are all exempt from the acquisition tax. So most of last year's acquisitions were tax-exempt.*

Most acquisitions → donations; donations → exempt. **Most + all = most.** Valid.

> *Most of the museum's acquisitions were donations. Most donations were sculptures. So most acquisitions were sculptures.*

Most + most, different subjects (acquisitions, donations). **Nothing follows** — not even "some." Flaw language: *"draws a conclusion about a group from two claims that each concern a majority of a different group."*
