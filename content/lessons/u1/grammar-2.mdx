## The habit that separates 175 from 165

Hard LSAT stimuli are hard because of *packaging*: nested clauses, abstract nouns, double negatives, academic hedges. Underneath is usually a simple structure. **Translation** is unpacking it into plain clauses — in your head, in a few seconds — before you reason about it.

> *It is far from clear that the absence of any documented instance of the species north of the river entails that its range has not, at some point, extended there.*

Translation: *No records north of the river. That doesn't prove it was never there.*

Now the argument is visible. Attack or defend as needed.

## Rules of translation

1. **One clause per idea.** Break long sentences at *and, but, which, because, unless*.
2. **Replace abstract nouns with verbs.** "The implementation of the policy" → "they implemented the policy." "A reduction in incidence" → "fewer cases."
3. **Resolve double negatives.** "Not uncommon" → "common." "Fails to rule out" → "might be." "It is not the case that no…" → "some…"
4. **Keep the scope words exactly.** Translation is allowed to simplify *packaging*, never *strength*. "Most" stays "most." "May" stays "may."
5. **Keep who said it.** "Critics claim X" is not "X." The author may disagree.

<KeyIdea>
Translate meaning, preserve force. A translation that turns "some" into "many" or "suggests" into "proves" has already picked a wrong answer.
</KeyIdea>

## Worked examples

<Example label="Hedged academic">
*Although the correlation between urban density and per-capita emissions is well established, it would be premature to conclude that policies promoting density will necessarily lower emissions in regions where transit infrastructure is lacking.*

→ *Dense cities emit less per person. But density policies might not cut emissions where there's no transit.*
</Example>

<Example label="Stacked negatives">
*No candidate who has not completed the ethics module is eligible unless granted an exemption.*

→ *Didn't do the module and no exemption → not eligible.* (Equivalently: eligible → did the module or has an exemption.)
</Example>

<Example label="Speaker attribution">
*Proponents of the tax insist that it will not reduce investment; the data, however, tell a different story.*

→ *Proponents say: no drop in investment. Author says: data disagree.* The author's position is the second clause.
</Example>

## Translating the stem

The question stem is also packaging. Translate it to the task:

| Stem | Task |
|---|---|
| "most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that" | name the flaw |
| "depends on assuming" | necessary assumption |
| "if assumed, enables the conclusion to be properly drawn" | sufficient assumption |
| "most seriously calls into question" | weaken |
| "most strongly supported by the information above" | what follows (modestly) |
| "most helps to explain" | resolve the discrepancy |

You'll internalize these; until then, say the task before reading the choices.

## Translation and the answer choices

Choices are packaged too. The correct answer to a Flaw question might read *"infers from the fact that a condition is necessary for an outcome that it is sufficient for that outcome."* Translate: *"treats a requirement as a guarantee."* Then check: did the argument do that?

Practice: for the next twenty questions you drill, write (or say) the one-line translation of the stimulus before looking at the choices. Prediction Mode in the drill settings enforces exactly this.
