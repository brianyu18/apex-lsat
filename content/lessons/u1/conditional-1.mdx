## The arrow

A **conditional** statement says: if one thing is true, another must be. We write it with an arrow.

> *If it rains, the match is cancelled.* → **rain → cancelled**

Left of the arrow is the **sufficient** condition: enough, by itself, to guarantee the right side. Right of the arrow is the **necessary** condition: required for the left side to hold — if it's absent, the left side can't be true.

Rain is *sufficient* for cancellation. Cancellation is *necessary* for rain (in this world, you can't have rain without a cancelled match).

## The only two valid moves

Given **A → B**:

| You learn… | You may conclude… | Name |
|---|---|---|
| A is true | B is true | following the arrow |
| B is false | A is false | the **contrapositive** (¬B → ¬A) |

And the two moves that feel valid but aren't:

| You learn… | You may conclude… | Error |
|---|---|---|
| B is true | *nothing* | affirming the necessary |
| A is false | *nothing* | denying the sufficient |

The match being cancelled doesn't tell you it rained — it could be lightning or a flood. No rain doesn't mean the match is on.

<KeyIdea>
A conditional only fires in two directions: forward from the sufficient, or backward from the *denial* of the necessary. Every other move is a flaw with a name.
</KeyIdea>

## Translating English into arrows

The sufficient condition isn't always first in the sentence. Learn the shapes:

| English | Arrow |
|---|---|
| If A, then B | A → B |
| B if A | A → B |
| A only if B | A → B |
| All A are B / Every A is B | A → B |
| No A is B | A → ¬B |
| A requires B / B is needed for A | A → B |
| Whenever A, B | A → B |

The trap is **"only if"**: it introduces the *necessary* condition. *"You may enter only if you have a ticket"* is **enter → ticket**, not ticket → enter.

## Why this matters beyond formal questions

Most Sufficient Assumption questions are conditional chains with one link missing. Many Flaw questions describe a conditional error in abstract language. Must Be True stimuli are chains you follow. And in Reading Comprehension, an author's "X is essential for Y" is a conditional you'll be asked to infer from. The next three lessons add the indicator words, the chaining rules, and the formal flaws — but the arrow and its two valid moves are 80% of the value.
