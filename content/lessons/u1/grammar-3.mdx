## The shift

A **relative** claim compares two things. An **absolute** claim describes one thing on its own.

- *Relative:* "Route A is faster than Route B."
- *Absolute:* "Route A is fast."

Neither follows from the other. Route A can be faster than B and still slow; both could be fast. The LSAT exploits this in three ways.

## 1. Shift in the argument (a flaw)

> *Hospitals that adopted the new protocol have lower infection rates than those that did not. So the protocol has made those hospitals safe from infection.*

Premise: lower *than others*. Conclusion: *safe*, an absolute. Flaw-answer language: *"treats a relative difference as if it established an absolute state."* Weaken-answer: *"Even hospitals with the protocol have infection rates well above the national target."*

## 2. Shift in the answer choice (a trap)

Stimulus: *Employees who took the training were more productive afterward.*
Tempting answer: *"Employees who took the training were highly productive."*
Killer: *"highly"* — the stimulus only supports *more productive than before*.

The reverse trap exists too: a stimulus establishes something absolute and an answer sneaks in a comparison that was never made.

## 3. Comparing the wrong things

> *This year's graduates applied to more jobs than last year's. So the job market is tougher this year.*

The comparison is between two cohorts' *behavior*; the conclusion is about the *market*. Maybe this year's cohort is simply larger, or applications got easier to submit. A valid comparison needs the same thing measured the same way on both sides.

<KeyIdea>
When you see *more, less, higher, better, faster, -er than* — stop and ask: compared with what? And does the conclusion stay on the comparison, or quietly jump to an absolute?
</KeyIdea>

## Percentages vs numbers

A special case you'll meet repeatedly:

> *The share of the budget spent on libraries fell from 4% to 3%. So the city cut library funding.*

A smaller **percentage** of a larger **total** can be a larger **number**. If the budget doubled, 3% is more money than 4% was. Any time a stimulus moves between *rate, share, proportion, percent* and *number, amount, total*, the gap is there. Flaw language: *"confuses a change in proportion with a change in absolute quantity."*

The same move hides in words like *per capita, rate, incidence, frequency* (ratios) versus *cases, incidents, people* (counts).

## Comparatives with missing baselines

- *"Twice as likely"* — twice a tiny risk is still tiny.
- *"Improved significantly"* — from what starting point?
- *"The most popular option"* — could still be chosen by a minority.
- *"Increasingly common"* — rising, but possibly still rare.

A correct Most Strongly Supported answer never upgrades these. A correct Flaw answer often names the missing baseline.

<Callout tone="warn" title="On the test">
When an answer choice converts a comparative into an absolute, or an absolute into a comparative, it is almost never the key. Circle the comparison words in the stimulus; the right answer keeps them.
</Callout>

## Quick drill

For each, is the conclusion relative or absolute, and does it match the premise?

1. *Model X scored higher on the crash test than Model Y. Therefore Model X is safe.* — premise relative, conclusion absolute: mismatch.
2. *Model X scored 92/100 on the crash test. Therefore Model X scored higher than most models.* — premise absolute, conclusion relative: mismatch (needs the distribution).
3. *Model X scored higher than Model Y. Therefore Model Y scored lower than Model X.* — both relative, same comparison: fine.
