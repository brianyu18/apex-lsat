## Negation is "it's not the case that"

To negate a statement, deny it as a whole — not flip every word in it. The smallest claim that contradicts the original is its negation.

| Statement | Negation | Not the negation |
|---|---|---|
| All A are B | **Some A are not B** (at least one) | No A are B |
| No A are B | **Some A are B** | All A are B |
| Some A are B | **No A are B** | Some A are not B |
| Most A are B | **At most half of A are B** (not most) | No A are B / Most A are not B |
| A → B | **A and not B is possible** | A → not B |
| X is always Y | X is sometimes not Y | X is never Y |
| X must be Y | X might not be Y | X cannot be Y |

<KeyIdea>
The negation of a universal claim is a single counterexample. The negation of "some" is "none." Overshooting the negation ("all" → "none") is the most common way people botch the negation test.
</KeyIdea>

## Why this matters: the negation test

On Necessary Assumption questions, the correct answer is one whose negation destroys the argument.

> *Argument: The new fertilizer increased yields in every trial plot. So it will increase yields on farms in the region.*

- Choice: *"Conditions on regional farms are not entirely unlike those in the trial plots."* Negated: *"Conditions are entirely unlike those in the plots."* Argument collapses. **Necessary.**
- Choice: *"Conditions on regional farms are identical to those in the trial plots."* Negated: *"Conditions are not identical."* The argument survives — similar is enough. **Not necessary.** Too strong.

Notice that negating correctly required knowing that the negation of "identical" is "not identical," not "completely different."

## "Some" answers are usually safe; "all" answers usually aren't

Necessary assumptions are modest, so the right NA answer often contains *at least some, not all, not every, not entirely*; wrong answers contain *all, every, only, always*. When two answers differ only in force, the weaker one is the necessary one. The reverse holds for Sufficient Assumption.

## Negating compound and conditional claims

- *"If the plan is adopted, costs will fall."* Negation: *"The plan could be adopted and costs not fall."* (Not: "if adopted, costs will rise.")
- *"Either the bridge or the tunnel will be funded."* Negation: *"Neither will be funded."*
- *"The policy is necessary and sufficient."* Negation: *"It is not necessary, or it is not sufficient."*

## "Not most" is not "few"

A stimulus says most residents support the measure. An answer says *"Most residents do not support the measure."* That's not the denial of the stimulus; it's a stronger opposing claim. The true denial is *"At most half support it."* Watch for this on Point at Issue and Must Be False questions, where the key is the statement that is *incompatible* with the stimulus — which can be weaker than its mirror image.

## Drill the reflex

For each, state the negation before reading on.

1. *Every applicant was interviewed.* → At least one applicant was not interviewed.
2. *None of the samples was contaminated.* → At least one sample was contaminated.
3. *Most voters prefer the incumbent.* → Half or fewer prefer the incumbent.
4. *The device works only when charged.* → The device can work when not charged.
5. *Some critics admired the film.* → No critic admired the film.
