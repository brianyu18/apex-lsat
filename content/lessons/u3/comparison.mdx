## The pattern

A comparative claim (*more, less, better, higher than*) is treated as an absolute claim (*many, good, high*), or a comparison is drawn with one side missing, the wrong baseline, or two things measured differently.

It is the single most common trap in **Most Strongly Supported** and **Must Be True**, and it appears in **Flaw**, **Weaken**, **Strengthen**, **Resolve/Explain**, **Necessary Assumption**, and **Evaluate**.

## How to recognize it

Every comparative fills three slots: *this*, *that*, *measured how*. Check each.

- **Relative to absolute.** "Earns more than" becomes "earns well." Higher than a low bar is still low.
- **Missing baseline.** *Lasts 30 percent longer, improved significantly, twice as likely.* Longer than what? Twice a trivial risk is trivial.
- **Wrong comparison class.** Compared with its own past, concluded against competitors. Compared with one rival, concluded against the field.
- **Different measures.** Hospital A's mortality against Hospital B's, when A takes the critical cases. Same word, different thing measured.
- **One side missing.** "A is better than B" argued from facts about A alone.

Also watch negative comparatives and superlatives: "no more X than Y" says nothing about whether either is X; "the most common cause" can account for a minority of cases.

## How it is exploited

| Question | What the key does | Abstract language |
|---|---|---|
| Flaw | Names the missing slot | "treats a relative difference as if it established an absolute standard" / "draws a comparative conclusion from evidence about only one of the things compared" / "compares X with Y without establishing that they are comparable" |
| Weaken | Fills the slot the wrong way | "Both programs' graduates earn below the median" / "Hospital A treats the region's most critical cases" |
| Strengthen | Shows like for like | "The two hospitals treat patients of similar severity" |
| Necessary Assumption | Supplies the other side | "[The other item] does not also have [the property]" |
| MSS / MBT | Keeps direction and class | The trap converts to an absolute, reverses the direction, or swaps the comparison class |
| Resolve / Explain | Reconciles two baselines | Per capita versus total; this year versus last; rate versus rank |

## The level-4 variant

Two comparisons to a third thing are treated as a comparison with each other. "A outperforms C, and B outperforms C" says nothing about A versus B. Or the class shifts silently between sentences: *than before* in the premise, *than others* in the conclusion, with no marker. Or a rate in one group is compared with a count in another.

The hardest **Must Be True** keys are valid relative-to-relative inferences that look like shifts: the weighted-average move in the spotter above, or "if A is taller than B and B is taller than C" chains. Train the rule, not the reflex: a relative conclusion derived entirely from relative premises on the same measure can be airtight.

<KeyIdea>
Every comparative is a three-slot claim: this, that, measured how. Confirm all three slots are filled in the premises, then confirm the conclusion keeps the same three or stays relative. A conclusion that drops to an absolute, swaps the class, or changes the measure is the flaw or the trap.
</KeyIdea>
