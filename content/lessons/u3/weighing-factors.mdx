## The pattern

An argument concludes that an option is better, worth it, or net harmful on the strength of one consideration (or one side's considerations), without accounting for what sits in the other pan.

It drives **Weaken** and **Strengthen** above all, plus **Flaw**, **Necessary Assumption**, **Evaluate**, and **Principle (Justify)**. It is a close cousin of value judgments, but the issue here is not that facts cannot reach an *ought*; it is that the ledger is incomplete.

## How to recognize it

The conclusion is comparative-evaluative: *should adopt, is preferable, is worth it, on balance, net benefit, outweighs, the advantages justify, the risk is unacceptable*. The premises list entries on one side only.

Three ledgers to check:

1. **The other pan.** A benefit cited, costs ignored; or a cost cited, benefits ignored.
2. **The alternative's ledger.** "A has drawback D, so choose B" assumes B lacks D. The status quo is also an option with costs.
3. **The units.** "Three thousand hectares against two million homes" is not a weighing until something converts the two into one measure. Counting entries is not weighing them.

## How it is exploited

| Question | What the key does | Abstract language |
|---|---|---|
| Weaken | Adds a heavy entry to the other pan, or shrinks the cited one | "The new route bypasses the hospital" / "The ten-minute saving applies only off-peak" |
| Strengthen | Closes the ledger | "No other effect of the change is significant" / "The alternative has the same drawback" |
| Flaw | Names the missing pan | "fails to consider the possible disadvantages of…" / "concludes that an option is preferable on the basis of a single consideration" |
| Necessary Assumption | Weak form only | "The benefit is not offset by any disadvantage of comparable size"; not "there are no disadvantages" |
| Evaluate | Asks for the other pan | "What would the alternative cost?" / "How large is the risk the policy would reduce?" |
| Principle (Justify) | Fixes the scale by fiat | "An option should be adopted whenever it [cited factor], regardless of its other effects" |

## The level-4 variant

The stimulus names both sides, so the ledger looks complete, and the gap is in the units or the alternative. "The program costs 2 million a year and has reduced recidivism by 4 percent; it is not worth continuing." Worth compared with what? Prisons cost something too. Or the argument tallies: "three advantages against one disadvantage," treating a count as a weight.

Necessary Assumption traps split on force: "has no drawbacks" is too strong for an "on balance" conclusion; "its drawbacks do not outweigh" is right. Flip it for an absolute conclusion ("is harmless"), where the strong form becomes necessary.

<KeyIdea>
An "on balance" conclusion is a claim about the entire scale. Ask three things: what is missing from the other pan, what the alternative's pan holds, and whether the two pans are measured in the same units. The correct answer almost always fills one of those three.
</KeyIdea>
