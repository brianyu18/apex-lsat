## The pattern

The conclusion is a verdict (*should, ought, right, wrong, justified, deserves, permissible, blameworthy*) and the premises are descriptions of what is the case. Something has to connect them: a value premise, a principle. When it is unstated, the argument assumes it.

This is the whole of **Principle (Justify)**, most of **Principle (Conform)**, and a large share of **Sufficient Assumption**, **Necessary Assumption**, **Flaw**, and **Point at Issue**. It is also why **Main Conclusion** answers so often carry a *should*.

## How to recognize it

Scan the conclusion for normative vocabulary, including the disguised kind: *a mistake, irresponsible, the best policy, unacceptable, commendable, has no business, is entitled to*. Then scan the premises. If none contains a *should*-type word or a stated principle, the bridge is missing.

Two refinements:

- **Modality.** *Permissible* is not *obligatory*; *should not be blamed* is not *should be praised*; *has a right to* is not *should*. A principle must deliver the exact verdict the conclusion asserts.
- **Prudential versus moral.** "To achieve G, do X" is a means claim, attacked by better means or side effects. "You ought to do X" without a goal is moral, attacked by competing values.

## How it is exploited

| Question | What the key does | Abstract language |
|---|---|---|
| Principle (Justify) | Supplies the principle, general enough to cover the case | "If [facts of the cited kind], then [the verdict]" |
| Sufficient Assumption | Same, but it must guarantee | Check the trigger matches the facts and the verdict matches the conclusion's modality |
| Necessary Assumption | Weakest bridge, or denies an override | "At least sometimes [facts] make [verdict] appropriate" / "No more important consideration requires otherwise" |
| Flaw | Names the gap | "draws a conclusion about what ought to be done from premises that describe only what is the case" / "presumes that [factual property] suffices to make an action [wrong]" |
| Principle (Conform) | Matches trigger and verdict type | A rule with the right verdict and the wrong trigger is the standard trap |
| Point at Issue | Finds the principle the speakers split on | They agree on the facts; the disagreement is "whether X should…" |

## The level-4 variant

The principle is present but mismatched. It licenses *permissible* when the conclusion says *required*; it covers legal duty when the conclusion is about moral duty; it covers *actions* when the conclusion judges a *person*. Reading the verdict's exact modality is the skill.

Second form: the value premise is dressed as a definition. "A good manager is one who retains staff. Ito retained all her staff. So Ito is a good manager." The definition carries the value; attack it there, not at the facts.

<KeyIdea>
Draw a line between the facts and the verdict. Nothing crosses without a principle. The right answer supplies the principle, denies that something overrides it, or names its absence, and the principle must deliver the conclusion's exact modality: permitted, required, or forbidden.
</KeyIdea>
