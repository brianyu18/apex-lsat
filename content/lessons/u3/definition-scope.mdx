## The pattern

A term appears in a premise and again in the conclusion, but with a different meaning (equivocation) or a different scope (the population it covers widens or narrows). The argument looks connected because the word repeats; the concepts do not match.

Contrast the link assumption, where different words hide a missing bridge. Here the same word hides it, which is harder to see.

It appears in **Flaw**, **Parallel Flaw**, **Necessary Assumption**, **Weaken**, **Point at Issue**, and as the main trap in **Most Strongly Supported** and **Must Be True**, where the answer quietly widens the stimulus's term. RC *meaning in context* questions test the same skill.

## How to recognize it

**Equivocation words.** The test's favorites carry a loose everyday sense and a strict technical one: *free, theory, law, natural, normal, healthy, safe, average, fair, public, represent, value, rational, interest, right*. When one of these appears twice, check whether both uses mean the same thing.

**Scope shifts.** A premise about *students at this school* becomes a conclusion about *students*; *the arts* becomes *painting*; *health* becomes *cardiovascular health*; *residents* becomes *the public*. Widening is the more common direction, but narrowing happens too.

**Stipulated definitions.** "By efficient, I mean…" fixes a meaning. The argument is fine while it keeps it, and flawed the moment the conclusion reverts to the everyday sense.

## How it is exploited

| Question | What the key does | Abstract language |
|---|---|---|
| Flaw | Names the slip | "relies on an ambiguity in the term X" / "uses a key term in two different senses" / "draws a conclusion about [broad class] from premises about [narrow class]" |
| Necessary Assumption | Pins the meaning or bridges the scope | "The sense in which X is [term] in the premise is the sense relevant to the conclusion" / "What holds for [narrow] holds for [broad]" |
| Weaken | Pulls the two senses apart in this case | "Subscription charges do not restrict what may be published" |
| MSS / MBT | Holds the stimulus's exact scope | The trap widens (*residents* to *the public*), narrows, or swaps a cousin (*costs* to *prices*) |
| Point at Issue / Agreement | Checks for a verbal dispute | Speakers using one word in two senses may not disagree, so "whether X" answers fail |
| Parallel Flaw | Matches equivocation with equivocation | A generic "unwarranted assumption" answer is the trap |

## The level-4 variant

The word stays fixed and the *referent* moves. "The company opposed the merger" (its management) becomes "the company benefits from the merger" (its shareholders). Or the shift is in what a quantifier attaches to: "the committee approved all proposals from finance" versus "all proposals the committee approved were from finance." Or in time: *currently* in the premise, *ever* in the conclusion.

Hardest of all is the defined term that drifts over three sentences: defined strictly, used strictly once, then used loosely in the conclusion, with the loose use feeling like the natural one.

<KeyIdea>
Every time a term repeats between premise and conclusion, check three things: same word, same meaning, same scope. If any one slips, you are looking at two arguments stapled together, and the right answer names the staple.
</KeyIdea>
