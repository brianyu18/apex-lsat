## The budget

| Paragraph | Minutes | Clock reads when done |
|---|---|---|
| Intro with thesis | 5 | 30:00 |
| Reason 1 | 8 | 22:00 |
| Reason 2 | 8 | 14:00 |
| Objection and reply | 8 | 6:00 |
| One-sentence close | 2 | 4:00 |
| Reread | 4 | 0:00 |

The outline sits beside the editor. Each paragraph is already planned; the minutes go to sentences.

## Sentence habits

- **Plain subject, plain verb.** "The rule removes housing" beats "It is the case that housing is removed by the rule." Short sentences are also the ones you can proofread.
- **No filler openers.** "In today's society," "Since the beginning of time," "It is widely agreed." Delete the opener and start at the claim.
- **"Because" and "for example" are structure words.** They announce that a reason or its support is arriving. Use them openly.
- **One idea per paragraph.** The first sentence states the reason; everything after supports it. A second idea belongs in the other reason paragraph or nowhere.

## Engaging a perspective without a strawman

Three moves, in order.

1. **Concede.** State the perspective at its strongest, in terms its holder would accept.
2. **Distinguish.** Show what it leaves out, or where it applies and where your case sits outside that range.
3. **Rebut.** Say why, given that, your thesis still holds.

<Example label="Objection paragraph, about 100 words">
Perspective C would cap rental permits instead of requiring hosts to live on site, and the appeal is real: a cap limits the loss of housing without dictating where anyone sleeps. *[Concede, in the holder's own terms.]* But a cap governs only how many rentals exist, not who runs them. *[Distinguish: what C leaves out.]* The complaints that moved the council were about absent owners rather than rentals as such: parties no one on site could stop, trash no one collected. *[Evidence for the distinction.]* An owner-occupancy rule answers both problems at once, because a resident host is one fewer investor and one more neighbor. *[Rebut; the thesis survives.]*
</Example>

## Behind at 20:00

At the 20-minute mark you should be finishing reason 2. If you are still inside reason 1, shorten: close it in two sentences, write reason 2 in four, and write the objection paragraph short, conceding in one sentence and rebutting in two. Never skip it. Engaging another perspective is the one move the task names, and an essay without it fails the assignment however good the first three paragraphs are. Cut the close to a single clause before you cut the objection.

## The reread

Four minutes, in this order.

1. Thesis in paragraph one, stated as a claim, not hedged.
2. Each body paragraph opens with its reason and contains concrete support.
3. The strongest opposing perspective is named, conceded, and answered.
4. No paragraph summarizes the prompt.
5. Filler openers and "I think" are gone.

Fix only what you find. Do not rewrite.

## Weekly practice

Run `/test/writing` once a week under both clocks with a prompt you have not seen. Then leave the essay alone and reread it the next day against the checklist above. A day later you read as the admissions reader does: with no memory of what you meant, only what you wrote. Mark the paragraph that drifted and the sentence you could not parse; those are next week's targets.

<KeyIdea>
Five paragraphs on a fixed budget: thesis by 30:00, each reason in eight minutes, the objection by 6:00, four minutes to reread. When behind, shorten the objection. Never drop it.
</KeyIdea>
