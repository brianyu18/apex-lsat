## The format

LSAT Argumentative Writing gives you a short context, a debatable issue, and three or four perspectives on it. Two clocks run in sequence: 15 minutes to analyze the prompt and plan, then 35 minutes to write. The task is fixed: take a position, support it with reasons, and engage at least one perspective other than your own. The essay is not scored. It is sent to every law school with your score report.

## Who reads it, and for what

An admissions reader spends a few minutes on it and asks two questions. Can this person build a clear argument under time, with no editor? And does the writing sound like the person who wrote the personal statement? The essay is a check against polished application materials. It does not need to be brilliant. It needs to be plainly competent, recognizably yours, and an argument.

## What strong essays do

- State a thesis in the first paragraph that is a claim, not a topic: "Calder Hollow should charge non-residents," not "Admission fees are a complex issue."
- Give two reasons, each in its own paragraph with something concrete under it: an example, a mechanism, or a consequence.
- Name the perspective that most threatens the thesis, concede what it gets right, and explain why the thesis survives.
- Close in one sentence.

## What weak essays do

| Habit | What the reader sees |
|---|---|
| Summarizing every perspective | A book report; no position until paragraph four, if ever |
| Hedging ("both sides have merit") | Someone who will not commit under mild pressure |
| Five sentences of throat-clearing | "Since the dawn of time, museums…" and the clock spent on nothing |
| Adopting a perspective and restating it | No reasoning of the writer's own |
| Skipping the objection | The one required move, missing |

## The five-paragraph plan

Thesis → reason 1 → reason 2 → objection and reply → one-sentence close. Four hundred to six hundred words. The shape is not a ceiling on good writing; it is a floor you can hit every time in 35 minutes without thinking about shape, which leaves the thinking for the argument.

## A model opening

<Example label="Opening paragraph, about 80 words">
The Calder Hollow museum should charge non-resident adults for admission. *[Thesis in sentence one: a subject, a verb, a claim.]* Residents already fund the museum through property taxes; visitors from outside the county use the same galleries and pay nothing toward them. *[Reason 1, previewed in one sentence.]* A modest fee closes most of the deficit without cutting the school programs that free admission was meant to protect. *[Reason 2, previewed.]* Perspective B is right that free access is the mission, but a fee on visitors who do not fund it is how that mission survives for the people who do. *[The objection named and the reply promised.]*
</Example>

Eighty words, four moves, no warm-up. Before paragraph two begins, the reader knows your position, both reasons, and which perspective you will answer.

<KeyIdea>
The essay is unscored but read. It asks for one thing: a committed claim, two supported reasons, and one honest objection answered, in five paragraphs. Decide the shape before you start so the clock goes to the argument.
</KeyIdea>
