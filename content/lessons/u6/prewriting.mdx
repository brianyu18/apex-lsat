## Why the first clock matters more

The 35 minutes go well or badly according to what you carry into them. A writer with a thesis, two supported reasons, and a chosen objection is transcribing. A writer without them is deciding and writing at once, and the essay drifts. Use the full 15 minutes; `/test/writing` lets you end the clock early, but you should rarely want to.

## The protocol

<Steps>
1. **Read (2 min).** Context, issue, every perspective. Note what each one wants and what it fears.
2. **Rank (2 min).** Which perspective is strongest, meaning hardest to argue against? Which is weakest? You will usually argue near the strongest and answer the second strongest.
3. **Commit (1 min).** One perspective, a combination, or your own. Choose the position you can defend, not the one you believe. Nobody is grading your convictions; they are reading your reasoning.
4. **Thesis (2 min).** One sentence, written as a claim.
5. **Two reasons with support (5 min).** Each reason answers "why?"; each support answers "how do you know?"
6. **Objection and reply (3 min).** Name the perspective that most threatens the thesis, write what it gets right, and write why your position holds anyway.
</Steps>

## "I think X" is not a thesis

"I think the town should require hosts to live on site" reports your state of mind, and a reader cannot disagree with a report. Delete the frame and state the claim with its grounds: "Pell Harbor should require short-term rental hosts to live on the property, because absentee rentals remove housing from residents and leave no one accountable for guests." Subject, verb, because. The outline check rejects a thesis that opens with "I think," and so should you.

## A reason is not a restatement

| Thesis | Restatement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Hosts should live on site | Owner-occupancy is the right policy for the town | Absentee rentals convert year-round housing into hotel rooms |
| Hosts should live on site | The town needs this rule | A resident host answers to neighbors in a way a management company does not |

Test: a reason could be false while the thesis stayed true. A restatement cannot.

## What counts as support

Each reason needs one of three things under it.

- **Example:** a concrete case. *On Tern Street, six of the nine houses sold since 2019 became rentals, and the elementary school lost a classroom's worth of enrollment.*
- **Mechanism:** why the reason works. *A host who sleeps in the building hears the party at midnight; a host in another state learns of it from a fine three weeks later.*
- **Consequence:** what follows from acting or not. *Without the rule, the next sale on every block is priced for an investor rather than a family, and prices ratchet up one house at a time.*

Invented details are fine; this tests reasoning, not research. Keep them specific.

## Which objection

Pick the perspective that most threatens your thesis, not the one easiest to knock down. Answering the weak perspective while ignoring the strong one reads as evasion. If Perspective C below solves the same problem with less intrusion, C is your objection, even though B is easier to dismiss.

<KeyIdea>
Prewriting is choosing: a defensible position, a thesis that is a claim, two reasons that could each be wrong, support under each, and the objection that actually threatens you. Every choice made now is one you do not make at minute 22.
</KeyIdea>
