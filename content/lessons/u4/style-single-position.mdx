## What the style looks like

The author has a claim and spends the passage making it. The thesis usually appears in the first paragraph, often right after a foil: a received view stated in one sentence so it can be overturned in the next ("is usually read as... This gets it backward"). What follows is the case: evidence, a worked example, an objection taken up and contained, and a consequence drawn at the end.

Because there is one voice, the difficulty moves. You will rarely be unsure what the author thinks. You will be asked what each paragraph *does* for the thesis, and how the whole is organized.

## How the author's position is signaled

- **The thesis sentence is assertive and early.** Look for the first sentence with no attribution and no hedge; everything after serves it.
- **The foil is marked as someone else's.** *Usually read as*, *is often taken to show*, *the conventional account*. One such phrase, then the author's turn.
- **Concessions are fenced.** *It is true that... But...* The concession paragraph grants a fact and denies that it damages the thesis; its role is concession, not counterpoint, because the author never lets the view stand.
- **The last paragraph cashes out.** *If the marks were rhetorical, then...* An implication paragraph extends the thesis; it does not restate it.

## Which questions it attracts

**Main point.** The thesis plus its reach. "Early punctuation encoded pauses for reading aloud rather than grammar" is the key. "Modern editors should disclose repunctuation" is the last paragraph, not the passage. "Aldric Vane's printings were consistent" is the example.

**Purpose of a paragraph.** The answer is a verb phrase describing the paragraph's job for the thesis: *provides evidence that the variation in the marks tracks breath rather than syntax*, not *describes Vane's printing practices*. Content words in the choice are allowed; the choice must still be about function.

**Organization.** The key is the role sequence stated abstractly: *a conventional interpretation is rejected in favor of an alternative; an example is analyzed; an objection is addressed; a consequence is drawn*. Check it clause by clause against your map. Organization traps usually get the first two clauses right and invent the third.

## The level-4 version

The hard single-position passage states the thesis in a qualified form ("not a grammar, or not only one") and then argues as if it were unqualified; the main point key keeps the qualification. Or the foil is never explicitly rejected, only outargued, and you must infer the author's stance from the example's design. In both cases the map still works: one voice, a sequence of jobs.

<KeyIdea>
In a single-position passage, find the thesis sentence and then read every paragraph as a job done for it. The questions ask what each paragraph does, in what order, and how far the thesis reaches.
</KeyIdea>
