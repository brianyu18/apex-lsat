## The stems

> *Which one of the following most accurately expresses the main point of the passage?*
> *The primary purpose of the passage is to...*
> *According to the passage, ...* / *The passage states that...*
> *Which one of the following most accurately expresses the meaning of "X" as it is used in the passage?*

Together these are about ten of the twenty-seven questions. They share a standard: the key is a claim the text establishes, and your job is to find the sentence or sentences that establish it. If you cannot point to them, you have not answered the question; you have recognized a choice.

## Main point: cover the whole passage, on the author's side

The key states the author's conclusion with its reach. Two tests. **Coverage**: does the choice account for every paragraph's job? A choice that is only paragraph two's claim fails, even if paragraph two is where the author turns. **Side**: is the choice what the author believes? The received view, accurately stated, is the most common wrong answer, because it is the sentence you read most carefully.

Prephrase from the map before you look: "Author rejects X for Y, and draws Z." Then find the choice that contains the rejection, the replacement, and the right scope words. The key is often the least vivid choice; vividness is where the overstatements live.

## Primary purpose: the main point as a verb

*To argue that...*, *to evaluate...*, *to reconcile...*, *to account for...* The verb must match the author's activity. A passage that advances a thesis is not "describing a debate"; a passage that weighs two explanations and declines to choose is not "arguing." Check the verb first, then the object. A right verb with the wrong object (half right) is the standard trap.

## Stated: the key paraphrases

The stem names a detail. Go to the paragraph your map assigns it, reread the sentence, and *then* read the choices. The key restates the sentence in different words; it never adds a reason, a comparison, or a consequence the sentence lacks. A choice that uses the passage's exact phrase is more suspect than one that doesn't, because exact phrases are how a nearby concept gets swapped in without your noticing.

## Meaning in context: the sentence's job decides

The word has several meanings; the question asks which one the sentence needs. Reread the sentence and ask what the word is doing there. "Clearinghouse" in "the principal clearinghouse for cloth and credit" means a place of exchange, because the sentence is describing what the fairs were for. The dictionary's most technical sense is the trap, and so is the most common sense when the passage is using a narrower one.

## The level-4 version

Hard main point questions put two choices on the author's side, both covering the passage, and separate them by one scope word: *primarily*, *in part*, *rather than*, *not only*. Hard stated questions ask about a detail stated across two sentences and paraphrase it so thoroughly that no word is shared with the text. Hard meaning questions pick an ordinary word ("record," "method") whose passage sense is slightly off the common one. The discipline is the same at every level: point to the sentence that proves the choice. If you cannot, keep looking.

<KeyIdea>
On these four types the key is a claim the text proves. Main point must cover every paragraph on the author's side; purpose must match the author's verb; stated answers paraphrase one sentence; meaning answers give the sense the sentence needs. Point to the proof before you select.
</KeyIdea>
