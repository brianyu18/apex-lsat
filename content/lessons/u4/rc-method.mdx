## What the questions actually test

A passage runs about 450 words and carries five to eight questions. Count what they ask: one main point, usually one purpose or organization, two or three about the author's or someone else's view, one or two about why a sentence is there, and perhaps two that ask what the passage says. Even the detail questions name the detail; you can look it up. Nothing rewards remembering the passage. Everything rewards knowing how it is built.

Reading for detail fails for a second reason: it spends attention that structure needs. A reader memorizing the dates in paragraph two is not noticing that paragraph two begins with *Yet*, and that word is the passage.

## The read: four tracks

Hold four things while you read, and nothing else.

1. **A low-resolution summary per paragraph.** Eight words or fewer, about what the paragraph *does*, not what it says: "Standard view and its appeal." "Author's rival explanation." "Evidence for it." Say it before you reach the paragraph's last sentence.
2. **Viewpoints.** Each time a view is attributed ("historians have long attributed," "critics contend," "it has been suggested"), note who holds it. A claim with a holder is not the author's claim until the author adopts it.
3. **The author's stance.** The author's voice enters through evaluation: *rightly*, *fails to*, *more plausibly*, *resists this account*, *tidy appeal*. Mark the first sentence that is the author's own. From then on, ask of every view: adopted, rejected, or merely reported.
4. **Pivots.** The words that change direction are the words that build the passage.

| Signal | Words | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | however, yet, but, although, nevertheless | the author is turning; the main point usually sits on this side |
| Concession | admittedly, granted, it is true that, to be sure | a point for the other side, about to be contained |
| Conclusion | thus, therefore, so understood, if this is right | the author is cashing out |
| Evaluation | rightly, mistakenly, tidy, overstated, better supported | the author's stance in one word |

## Why structure wins

A main point answer must cover the whole passage and sit on the author's side; the map gives you both. A "primarily in order to" question asks what a detail does for its paragraph; the paragraph's summary is the answer. An attitude question asks for the evaluative words; you marked them. The only questions the map cannot settle alone are the ones that send you back to a sentence, and it tells you which paragraph that sentence is in.

## The map is the passage at low resolution

When you finish, you should be able to say without looking: what the standard view is, who holds it, where the author broke from it, what the author's explanation is, what supports it, and what follows. Six items. That is the passage. The rest is where you go to confirm.

<KeyIdea>
Read for the shape: what each paragraph does, who holds each view, and where the author's own voice enters. Details are retrievable; structure is not, and most questions ask about structure.
</KeyIdea>
