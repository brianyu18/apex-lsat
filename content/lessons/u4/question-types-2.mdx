## The stems

> *The passage most strongly suggests that the author would be most likely to agree with which one of the following?*
> *The passage suggests that [the critics] would be most likely to agree that...*
> *The author's attitude toward X can most accurately be described as...*
> *The author mentions X primarily in order to...*
> *The primary function of the second paragraph is to...*

Eight or nine per section, and the group where 170 and 175 part ways. These keys are not stated in the text. They are *supported* by it, and the standard is strict.

## "Most strongly suggests": the modest claim wins

Treat it as Most Strongly Supported from LR: the key is the claim you could defend to a hostile reader using only the passage.

- **The key is smaller than you expect.** "Warming alone cannot account for the decline" is provable. "Warming played no role" is not; the passage said the causes need not be rivals.
- **Hedges carry over.** *Better supported* licenses *more plausible*, never *demonstrated*.
- **Perspective is per person.** For an other's-perspective question, use only that camp's sentences. The author's reasons do not belong to the critics, even when they share a conclusion.
- **Negative inferences are legal.** If the author rejects X because X fails test T, the author would agree that any explanation must pass T. The passage never says so; it proves it.

## Attitude: calibrate the word

Attitude keys are usually two words, one of which is a qualifier. Build the scale and place the author on it from the evaluative words you marked.

| Text says | Attitude key | Not |
|---|---|---|
| "defends it, though it leaves open..." | qualified endorsement | enthusiastic support |
| "deserve reclassification, not rehabilitation" | measured appreciation | unreserved admiration |
| "tidy appeal... yet the chronology resists" | respectful skepticism | contempt |
| "has been repeated more often than tested" | pointed disapproval | neutrality |
| "the evidence is correlational" | cautious acceptance | conviction |

A qualifier that appears nowhere in the text is a trap in either direction: *grudging* when the author is not reluctant, *wholehearted* when the author withholds.

## Function: say what it does, not what it says

Purpose-in-context and purpose-of-paragraph keys are verb phrases about the argument: *to concede a limitation before showing it does not affect the conclusion*. The commonest trap describes the content accurately (*to describe Vane's printing practices*) and so feels true. It is true. It is not a function. Ask what the paragraph would lose if the sentence were cut; that loss is the function.

For a paragraph, the map already has the role; the key is that role with the paragraph's object attached. For a sentence, reread it with the sentence before it, which usually makes the claim the target sentence serves.

## The level-4 version

The hard perspective question is provable only by combining two paragraphs: the author's criterion from one, a fact from another. The hard attitude question offers two qualified phrases separated by the qualifier's direction (*cautious* versus *reluctant*). The hard function question targets a sentence whose job is for the *next* paragraph. The discipline holds: find the sentences that prove it, and reject any choice that needs one you cannot find.

<KeyIdea>
Inference keys are the smallest claims the text supports, attributed to the right speaker. Attitude keys use the qualifier the text pays for. Function keys say what a sentence or paragraph does for the argument, not what it is about.
</KeyIdea>
