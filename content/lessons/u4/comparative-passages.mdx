## What you are given

One set per section: passages A and B, 200 to 280 words each, on one subject, by authors who never read each other. Mapping each is necessary, not sufficient; the product of the read is one sentence: how B stands to A.

## The four relationships

|Relationship|A|B|Key phrasing|
|---|---|---|---|
|Generalization / instance|a general claim|one case|"B describes an example of what A discusses"|
|Principle / application|a rule|a case judged by it|"B applies a principle A articulates"|
|Point / counterpoint|a thesis|a rejection of it|"B challenges a conclusion A defends"|
|Two angles|one aspect|another aspect|"Both concern X; A its causes, B its effects"|

Real sets blur the lines: an instance can *qualify* a generalization, row one leaning toward row three. State the lean; the key will.

<Example label="A short pair">
**Passage A.** Price ceilings on rental housing are among the few policies economists across schools agree about: by holding rents below the market rate, they reduce the return on building and maintaining rental units, and over time the supply falls. The effect may take years, but its direction is not in doubt. A municipality that adopts rent control has chosen a smaller rental stock.

**Passage B.** Harwick adopted rent stabilization in 1998, and its rental stock has since grown faster than any comparable city's. The drafters exempted every building completed after enactment and let owners reset rents on vacancy. Critics predicted a construction slowdown; builders, facing no ceiling on new units, built. Harwick suggests not that the standard analysis is wrong but that it describes a design choice, not a policy.

**Relationship.** An instance that qualifies A's generalization: B accepts A's mechanism and denies A's conclusion that adopting rent control *is* choosing a smaller stock. A's likely reply, that an ordinance exempting new construction is no ceiling on the units it would deter, is the next question.
</Example>

## Two ways to read

**Sequential.** Map A. Read B *against* A: at each paragraph, does B agree, disagree, narrow, or ignore A? Write the relationship sentence, then do all the questions. This is the default; most comparative questions involve both passages, and the sentence answers several.

**Split.** Map A, answer the A-only stems, then read B and do the rest. Split wins when a ten-second scan shows two or more A-only stems, or when A is dense enough that its details will not survive B. With zero or one, switching costs more than it gains.

## The comparative question types

- **Relationship.** The key is true of each passage *as a whole*; a choice that fits A exactly and B loosely is wrong.
- **Approach.** *Both passages are primarily concerned with...* must hold for each passage alone. *Only passage A...* names something B never does, not something B does less.
- **Cross-perspective.** *The author of passage B would most likely regard the claim in A's final sentence as...* Find B's sentence that bears on that claim; the attitude is there, not in B's mood. Here "a design choice, not a policy" makes A's last sentence *overstated*, not *false*.

## The level-4 version

Two passages reach the same conclusion by incompatible reasoning, or disagree while sharing every premise. The relationship key is then about reasoning, and cross-perspective questions ask whether A would accept B's *argument*, not B's conclusion. Map each passage's conclusion and reason separately; compare line to line.

<KeyIdea>
Read B against A and state in one sentence how B stands to A before any both-passage question. Relationship keys must hold for both passages as wholes; cross-perspective keys come from the sentence in one passage that bears on the other's claim.
</KeyIdea>
