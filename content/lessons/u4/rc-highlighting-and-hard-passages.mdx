## Highlighting on the real interface

The testing interface gives you a highlighter and an underline tool, and the passage stays on screen beside every question. Marks are not study notes; they are flags, so that when a question sends you back your eye lands on the right sentence in two seconds instead of ten.

Flags work when sparse; a half-yellow paragraph has none. Mark words, and only these kinds:

|Mark|Why|
|---|---|
|Pivots: *however*, *yet*, *although*, *nevertheless*|the structure turns; the main point is downstream|
|Attributions: *critics contend*, *it has been suggested*|who owns the next claim|
|Evaluative words: *rightly*, *overstates*, *tidy*, *better supported*|the author's stance in one word|
|Conditionals and quantifiers: *unless*, *only if*, *most*|the exact force a key must preserve|
|Quoted or defined terms|meaning-in-context targets|
|Concession openers: *it is true that*, *admittedly*|the containment that follows is the point|

Do not mark names, dates, examples, or whole sentences. Names and dates are found by scanning; a highlighted sentence is content, and content is not what you are reading for.

## The level-4 passage

Hard passages are hard in three ways.

**The subject is itself an argument.** A distinction, a method, an objection to an objection; nothing to picture. Response: translate each abstract sentence into a plain one before moving on, keeping the force and dropping the packaging.

<Example label="A level-4 sentence, translated">
*The objection that the distinction between interpreting a text and applying it cannot be sustained, because every application presupposes an interpretation, is correct as far as it goes; but it does not follow that the distinction is useless, only that it is one of degree, and a distinction of degree is precisely what a doctrine of deference requires, since deference is itself a matter of degree.*

Translation: *The objection's premise is right; its conclusion isn't. The distinction is one of degree, and so is deference.*

Marks: *objection*, *correct as far as it goes; but*, *does not follow*, *degree*.
</Example>

**The author endorses partially.** A premise accepted and its conclusion rejected; a camp joined for a different reason; two views ranked rather than chosen. Response: map each view's conclusion and reason separately and record the author's stance on each; the perspective question asks about one and offers the other as the trap.

**Inferences span paragraphs.** The key to a hard perspective question joins the author's criterion from one paragraph with a fact from another; neither states it. Response: the criterion lives where the author dispatched a rival, the fact in a support or concession paragraph. Side by side, the inference is one step.

## Answer from the map, confirm with the text

1. Prephrase from the map before reading the choices.
2. Find the match; eliminate what contradicts the map.
3. Go to the text and find the sentence that proves the survivor: the paraphrased sentence for stated, the sentence or pair the inference rests on for perspective.
4. No proof in twenty seconds means you selected a memory, not an answer; reread the paragraph the map points to.

Step three is what 170-scorers skip under time. It costs ten seconds where the map was right and saves the point where it was slightly wrong, which on a hard passage is often.

If you finish a hard passage unable to say what the author concluded, do not reread it all. Reread the last paragraph and the sentence after each marked pivot; the author's voice concentrates there.

<KeyIdea>
Highlight structure words, not content sentences. On a hard passage, translate abstract sentences, record each view's conclusion and reason separately, and pair paragraphs for inferences. Prephrase from the map; select only what the text proves.
</KeyIdea>
