## What the style looks like

A problem is stated, with enough detail to show why it is hard. Proposed remedies follow, each measured against something. Then the author's own remedy, or a verdict on the field, and often a closing principle. Law supplies most of these; policy and institutional history supply the rest.

The thing to find is the **criterion**: the standard the author uses to judge every remedy. It is rarely announced. It shows up as the reason the first remedies fall short ("without reaching its cause") and again as the reason the author's remedy works ("places the burden on the party with better access to the facts"). Once you have it, the principle and application questions are already answered.

## How the author's position is signaled

- **The diagnosis is the author's.** When the passage says what the problem's *cause* is, the author is speaking, and the remedies will be judged by whether they reach that cause.
- **Remedies are dispatched by one phrase each.** *Eases the problem without reaching its cause.* A dispatched remedy is a counterpoint even when the author concedes it helps.
- **The author's remedy carries a reason.** The reason is the principle.
- **Objections are answered, not ignored.** *Real but overstated.* A concession paragraph in this style takes the strongest objection to the author's remedy and limits it.

## Which questions it attracts

**Purpose in context.** *The author mentions that the officials who adopted the practice have left primarily in order to...* Go back to the sentence and ask what it does for the paragraph: it sharpens the problem by showing the order now binds people who did nothing. Not "to describe how institutions change." The key names a function the paragraph needed.

**Principle.** *Which one of the following principles underlies the author's reasoning?* The key is the criterion at the author's level of generality: the burden of justifying a continuing constraint belongs to the party it benefits. Two traps: a principle so broad the passage cannot support it ("courts should never issue indefinite orders") and a restatement of the specific remedy dressed as a principle ("injunctions should include sunset clauses").

**Application.** A new case, and the question is whether the author's criterion is met. A licensing board that must renew a restriction annually by showing continued need fits. A rule that lets the restricted party petition for release does not; the inertia still favors the restriction. Test the case against the criterion, not against surface resemblance to injunctions.

## The level-4 version

The hard version offers three remedies and endorses a combination, or endorses none and ends on the criterion alone. The main point is then the criterion, and the application question gives you a case that satisfies one remedy's letter while violating the criterion. Map the criterion as its own line.

<KeyIdea>
In a problem-analysis passage, extract the criterion the author uses to judge remedies. It is the answer to the principle question, the test for the application question, and the reason behind every "primarily in order to."
</KeyIdea>
