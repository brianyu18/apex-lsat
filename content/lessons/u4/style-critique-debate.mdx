## What the style looks like

A position is introduced, usually with its appeal. Objections or a rival position follow. Then the author rules: adopts one side, splits the difference, or rejects the terms of the dispute. Law and social science supply most of these passages, because those fields have named camps.

The tells arrive early. Paired attribution verbs in the first paragraph ("has been defended as... and attacked as"). Camp nouns: *critics*, *defenders*, *proponents*, *skeptics*. And a late paragraph that opens with a pivot and a shift to the author's own voice.

## How the author's position is signaled

- **Who gets the last word.** The paragraph after the last reported view is the verdict. Until it arrives, every paragraph is somebody else's.
- **Evaluative verbs attached to a camp.** *Proves less than it seems*, *have the better of it*, *overstates*, *is sound*. One verb settles a camp's fate.
- **Re-grounding.** The author agrees with a camp's conclusion but replaces its reason. This is where 175-level questions live: the key to an author's-perspective question states the author's reason, and a trap states the camp's.

Read the debate below for exactly that. The author ends on the defenders' side. Watch whether the author accepts the defenders' *argument*.

After you check: both camp paragraphs are counterpoints. A paragraph that reports a view is not support for the author, even when the author later lands near that view. The verdict is the only paragraph the author owns.

## Which questions it attracts

| Type | What the key must do here |
|---|---|
| Author's perspective | state the author's reason: a scheme does not show the legislature considered *this* remedy |
| Other's perspective | state a camp's own claim: defenders would say the cost of an express clause is small |
| Author's attitude | calibrate: *qualified endorsement*, not *unreserved approval*; the last clause withholds something |
| Main point | the verdict, not the dispute: "the presumption survives the critics' charge, on a different ground" |

The trap on the other's-perspective question is the author's re-grounding attributed to the defenders. The defenders never said "evidence of a decision actually made"; the author did. The trap on the attitude question is a stronger word than the text pays for: the author *defends* the rule and immediately names what the defense leaves open.

## The level-4 version

The hard debate passage has three positions, not two, and the author's verdict borrows from each. Or the author concedes the critics' premise and denies their conclusion, as here: yes, a detailed scheme means the legislature thought about remedies; no, that does not mean it thought about this one. Keep the premise and the conclusion separate in your map, because the question will.

<KeyIdea>
In a debate passage, every reported paragraph is a counterpoint until the author's own arrives. Track each camp's conclusion and its reason separately; the author can adopt one without the other, and the questions test which.
</KeyIdea>
