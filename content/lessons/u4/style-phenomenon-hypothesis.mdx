## What the style looks like

An observation that "demands an explanation." Then hypotheses in turn, each tested against a fact. Then the author's verdict, usually hedged: *better supported*, *more consistent with*, *the evidence is correlational*. Natural science owns this style, but economics and history use it too.

The passage is an argument from elimination, and its spine is the **discriminating fact**: the one observation that each hypothesis must fit and only one does. Find it. Every reasoning question about the passage turns on it.

## How the author's position is signaled

- **A hypothesis paragraph ends with its verdict.** *The difficulty is...*, *cannot by itself account for...*, *confirm the preference*. Tag the paragraph by that verdict: a rejected hypothesis is a counterpoint; an adopted one is support for the author's conclusion.
- **The hedge is part of the position.** "Better supported, though correlational" is not the same claim as "established." The author's-perspective key preserves the hedge; a trap strips it.
- **"Need not be rivals."** Authors often reserve a role for the losing hypothesis. Do not map "rejected" when the text says "secondary."

The discriminating fact: neighboring islands warmed equally but have no rats and no decline. The climatic hypothesis is rejected for failing it, not for being false. The author even keeps a role for warming in the last paragraph. Your map should record that, because a perspective question will ask whether the author thinks warming played *no* part.

## Which questions it attracts

**Author's perspective.** *The passage most strongly suggests that the author would agree that...* The key is modest and hedged like the text: "warming alone cannot account for the decline." Traps: "warming played no role" (the author says the causes need not be rivals), "predation has been shown to cause the decline" (the author says correlational).

**Weaken, strengthen, evaluate.** The target is the author's reason for preferring predation: specificity to Perrin. A weakener attacks it. "Rats reached a neighboring island in the same decade, and its skinks did not shrink" removes the discriminating fact. A strengthener adds a prediction the hypothesis makes and the rival does not: sites where rats were eradicated show body length recovering. An evaluate question asks what you would need to know, and the answer is a fact that differs between the hypotheses, such as whether rat-free sites on Perrin show the decline. A fact true under both hypotheses, such as whether skinks eat insects, evaluates nothing.

**Application.** A new case, and the question is which observation the author's method treats as decisive. Pick the observation that separates the candidate causes, not the one that is merely consistent with the favored cause.

## The level-4 version

Three hypotheses, two of which survive, and a verdict that ranks them by what each leaves unexplained. The questions ask what the author concedes to the second-place hypothesis. Map each hypothesis with the fact it fails, not just a thumbs down.

<KeyIdea>
A phenomenon-hypothesis passage is an argument from elimination. Find the discriminating fact, map each hypothesis by the fact it fails, and keep the author's hedge; weaken, strengthen and perspective questions all live on that fact.
</KeyIdea>
