## The stems and the task

Disagreement: *"[A] and [B] disagree over whether"* or *"The dialogue most supports the claim that [A] and [B] disagree about whether."* Agreement: *"[A] and [B] are committed to agreeing about which one of the following?"*

The task is identical in both: for each choice, determine what **each** speaker would say about it, using only what they said. Disagreement needs one Yes and one No. Agreement needs two of the same. Anything else is out.

## What the right answer looks like

- A proposition, phrased neutrally, that one speaker **affirms** and the other **denies** on the record.
- Usually it is the first speaker's conclusion or the principle under it, because that is what the second speaker engages.
- For Agreement: typically a background fact or premise both rely on, often one the second speaker silently accepts in order to argue from it.
- Both cells must be filled from the text. "Would probably agree" is a question mark.

## The level-4 version

- **The second speaker states no conclusion.** They add a fact or ask a question. You infer their position from what the evidence would be *for*: Teo's facts only make sense as reasons against return.
- **They agree on the conclusion and disagree about the reasoning.** Both think the panels should go back; one denies that consent is the reason. The credited answer is about the premise, and the conclusion-shaped choice becomes the trap.
- **Agreement with a twist.** The credited answer is a premise the second speaker never states but must accept to make their reply relevant. Fill the cell by asking: if they denied this, would their reply still make sense?

<KeyIdea>
A point at issue is a sentence one speaker would affirm and the other would deny, based only on what each said. Fill both grid cells from the text; a question mark in either cell kills the choice, no matter how central the topic feels.
</KeyIdea>

## Before you read the choices

1. One line per speaker: their claim, inferred if unstated.
2. Mark what the second speaker's words actually engage.
3. Draft the crux as a neutral "whether" clause; then grid every choice.
