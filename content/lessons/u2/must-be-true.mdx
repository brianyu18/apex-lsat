## The stems and the task

> *If the statements above are true, which one of the following must also be true?* — Must Be True
> *If the statements above are true, which one of the following CANNOT be true?* — Must Be False
> *Which one of the following can be properly inferred from the statements above?* — Inference

All three are deductive. **Must be true**: the choice holds in every situation consistent with the facts. **Must be false**: the choice fails in every such situation — its negation must be true. **Properly inferred** means validly deduced; treat it as Must Be True. Probable, plausible, and likely do not count. The test for any choice is the counter-scenario: can you build a world where the facts hold and the choice doesn't?

## What the right answer looks like

- A valid deduction, usually from two or more statements chained through a shared term — frequently a contrapositive.
- Often weaker than you expect: *some*, *at least one*, *not all*. Never reject a choice for being weak.
- No new terms. A word without a referent in the facts can't be deduced.
- For Must Be False, the right answer contradicts something derivable. Strong wording in the choice is fine, because the facts rule it out.
- Quantifier arithmetic: *all* + *some* gives *some*; *most* + *most* on the same group gives *some*; *some* + *some* gives nothing.

## The level-4 variant

Level-4 Must Be True buries the chain: four or five statements, two of them irrelevant, the key link written as *unless* or *except*, and the answer stated as a contrapositive with negated terms — *No proposal lacking an outreach component was funded* rather than *Every funded proposal had one*. Hard Must Be False choices are compounds (*a funded proposal from a first-time applicant that requested $60,000*), where only the conjunction is impossible. Hard Inference stems mix quantities with conditionals: *most* of a group satisfies a rule that triggers a second rule. Diagram, chain, and run the counter-scenario test on the two finalists.

Keep the MSS line clear: on Most Strongly Supported one counter-scenario does not kill a well-supported choice. Here it does.

<KeyIdea>
The only test is the counter-scenario. For Must Be True, try to make the choice false while every fact stays true; for Must Be False, try to make it true. If you can, it's out. The answer is the choice you cannot break.
</KeyIdea>

## Before you read the choices

1. Diagram every conditional and its contrapositive; mark quantities.
2. Chain through shared terms; write the two or three things that follow.
3. Reread the stem — *must be true*, *CANNOT be true*, or *properly inferred* — and hold the matching test in mind.
