## The stem and the task

> *The argument depends on assuming which one of the following?* / *Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?*

Find a statement the argument **cannot do without**. Not one that would help; not one that proves the conclusion; one whose falsity breaks the argument. That gives you the decisive tool, the **negation test**: negate the choice and reread. If the argument now fails, the choice was required. If it limps along, it wasn't.

## What the right answer looks like

- **Modest.** *At least some*, *not all*, *does not*, *there is no other*. The argument needs a plank, not the whole bridge.
- Two shapes. A **bridge**: the weakest link between a premise term and the conclusion term. A **defender**: a statement ruling out one specific way the conclusion could fail.
- It can sound trivial. "The cloth was dyed at all" is a legitimate necessary assumption.
- Negated, it doesn't merely weaken the argument — it severs it.
- Never stronger than the argument needs. A choice the author would love but doesn't require is the classic wrong answer.

## Negating correctly

Negate the force, not the content.

| Choice says | Negation |
|---|---|
| all / every X are Y | not all X are Y |
| some X are Y | no X are Y |
| most X are Y | half or fewer X are Y |
| X will happen | X will not necessarily happen |
| X is the only cause | X is not the only cause |
| if A then B | A can occur without B |

The negation of *all* is not *none*, and the negation of *most* is not *few*. Over-negating makes unnecessary answers look necessary.

## The level-4 variant

At level 4 the required assumption rules out a possibility you never thought of — so when your prephrased defenders don't appear, run the negation test instead of hunting for your own words. The assumption may sit between a premise and a sub-conclusion rather than at the main conclusion. The best trap is a sufficient assumption that sounds like a complete bridge; it fails negation because *not all* leaves the argument intact. The second trap uses the right concept with the wrong quantity: *most* where the argument needs only *some*. Expect the correct answer to be a negative sentence that looks too small to matter.

<KeyIdea>
Necessary means the argument dies without it. Negate the choice precisely — flip the force, not the content — and keep only the one whose negation leaves the conclusion unsupported.
</KeyIdea>

## Before you read the choices

1. Conclusion and the premise that carries it.
2. Three ways the conclusion could be false with the premise true — these are the defenders you need.
3. The conclusion term missing from the premise — the bridge you need, at minimum strength.
