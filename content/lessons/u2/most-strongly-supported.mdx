## The stem and the task

> *Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?* / *The statements above, if true, most strongly support which one of the following?*

Usually no argument — a set of facts, and you draw the conclusion. The standard is **well supported**, not guaranteed: the answer the facts make clearly probable, with no step the facts don't license. In practice the right answer is anchored in the text and the wrong answers are unsupported, not merely "less supported." Hold a text standard, not a plausibility standard.

## What the right answer looks like

- It **combines** two or more statements that share a term. One statement alone rarely supports anything new.
- Its force matches the weakest fact it rests on: *rarely* in, *rarely* (or *seldom*, *usually not*) out — never *never*.
- It introduces no new concept. A noun with no referent in the stimulus disqualifies the choice.
- It often sounds underwhelming. The hedged, cautious answer you'd call obvious is usually right.
- Conditionals are honored exactly: *only when* gives a necessary condition and its contrapositive, nothing more.

## MSS versus Must Be True

| | Must Be True | Most Strongly Supported |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | true in every world where the facts hold | clearly probable given the facts |
| What kills a choice | one consistent counter-scenario | a step the text doesn't license |
| Typical answer | a deduction, often a contrapositive | a cautious combination, often hedged |

A choice supported by *rarely* and *often* can be right on MSS and would not be right on MBT. A choice that needs an outside fact fails both.

## The level-4 variant

At level 4 the stimulus is a mini-argument and the supported claim is the conclusion the author stopped short of stating — or a negative one: *not all X are Y*, *at least some X fail to Z*. Quantifier chains appear: *most A are B* and *most A are C* support *some B are C*; *some* and *some* support nothing. The best trap is supported by common sense rather than by the text; it reads as obvious and has no sentence behind it. The second-best trap is right except for one force word. When two choices both seem supported, pick the one with fewer added steps — the one closer to the page.

<KeyIdea>
Every word in the right answer must be paid for by a sentence in the stimulus. "Most strongly supported" relaxes certainty, not anchoring: the answer may be probable rather than guaranteed, but it may not be imported.
</KeyIdea>

## Before you read the choices

1. Inventory the facts; note which pairs share a term.
2. Chain the pair that shares a term; state what follows, with the weaker force word.
3. Note the one thing the facts tempt you to conclude but don't — usually the reversed conditional.
